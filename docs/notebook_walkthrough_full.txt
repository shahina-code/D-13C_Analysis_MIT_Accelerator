"STALE" is important and appears a lot. A stale cell is one where somebody
edited the code but never re-ran it, so the output saved in the .ipynb file
was produced by an OLDER version of the code. The output you see on screen is
not what the code on screen would produce. There are several of these.


================================================================================
   PART 0 — THE PHYSICS, IN PLAIN LANGUAGE
================================================================================

WHAT WE ARE TRYING TO MEASURE
A beam of deuterons (heavy hydrogen nuclei, "d") is fired at a carbon-13
target. Sometimes a deuteron fuses with a 13C nucleus:

        d + 13C  ->  14C + p          Q = +5.951 MeV

That reaction spits out a proton. We want to know how often this happens —
the cross-section. To get that we need to count those protons.

THE PROBLEM: THAT REACTION IS RARE
The target chamber is full of deuterium, so far and away the most common
reaction is deuterons hitting each other:

        d + d  ->  T + p              proton comes out at 3.02 MeV

This happens roughly 100,000 times for every one 13C+d event. So we are
looking for a needle in a haystack, and the haystack is made of the same
material as the needle (both are protons).

THE TWO DETECTORS

  SBD (Silicon Barrier Detector)
      An electronic detector. Every proton that hits it produces a voltage
      pulse whose height is proportional to the proton's energy. So the SBD
      gives us an ENERGY SPECTRUM: a histogram of "how many protons at each
      energy". Different reactions make different-energy protons, so they show
      up as separate peaks. Sits 4.0 cm from the target, at 90 degrees to the
      beam, behind a small aperture of area 0.172 cm^2.

  CR-39 (plastic nuclear track detector)
      A piece of clear plastic. A proton punching through it leaves a trail of
      broken molecules. Soak the plastic in hot NaOH ("etching") and those
      trails open up into little conical pits you can see under a microscope.
      A scanner photographs the whole piece and tabulates every pit.
      Sits at ~125 degrees to the beam, behind a 125-50 um tantalum filter.

      CRUCIAL: CR-39 does NOT measure energy. It gives you a pit's DIAMETER,
      DARKNESS (contrast) and OVAL-NESS (eccentricity), and its x,y position.
      Diameter correlates with energy but is not a clean energy measurement.
      This is why the question "where is the CR-39 peak" needs the careful
      answer given in Part 5.

      The Ta filter is the trick: it is thick enough to completely stop the
      3.02 MeV D+D protons, but thin enough that the faster 5.6 MeV 13C+d
      protons get through. So in principle the CR-39 only ever sees signal.
      In practice this is not what we observe — see Problem #1.

THE STRATEGY
Count the D+D protons (easy, there are millions) and count the 13C+d protons
(hard, there are a few dozen). The ratio of the two, times the known D+D
cross-section, gives the 13C+d cross-section. The absolute beam current and
target thickness cancel out in the ratio, which is why it is done this way.

CELL 7 — markdown: "is the 5 MeV peak real?"
  Lays out three reasons to be suspicious of the 4.838 MeV peak:
    (a) POSITION. Two D+D protons at 2.457 MeV summed together give
        2 x 2.457 = 4.914 MeV. Observed 4.838. That is close.
    (b) WIDTH. If you add two independent measurements each of width 0.0578,
        the sum has width sqrt(2)*0.0578 = 0.082. Observed 0.094. Also close.
    (c) ENERGY DOESN'T MATCH. The 13C(d,p)14C proton should come out at
        Q * 14/15 = 5.55 MeV, not 4.84. The peak is in the wrong place by
        0.7 MeV, which is far too big to be a calibration error.
  Then it states the decisive test, which is the key idea of the whole
  notebook:

        A REAL nuclear line is produced by ONE particle. Its count is
        proportional to the beam rate R.  N ~ R.
        A PILE-UP peak needs TWO particles arriving close together. Its count
        is proportional to R squared.  N ~ R^2.

        So take the ratio  N_peak / N_(2.46 MeV line).
        For a real line that ratio is FLAT as you change the rate.
        For pile-up that ratio RISES LINEARLY with rate.

  We have 54 runs at different beam currents, so we can just measure it.

CELL 8 — THE PILE-UP TEST
  DATA IN  re-reads all 54 CH2 .root files, plus each run's _info.txt to get
           that run's live time (parsed out of the CH2 block with a regex).
  LOGIC    Four energy windows are defined:
               LOW      2.25 - 2.65 MeV   the intense D+D line
               PILEUP   4.60 - 5.05 MeV   the suspect peak
               SIGNAL   5.08 - 5.38 MeV   where 13C+d SHOULD be
               SIDE     5.60 - 6.60 MeV   flat continuum, for background
           For each run: count events in each window, and compute
               rate = N_LOW / live_time
           Sort the 54 runs by rate. Pool them into 4 groups chosen so each
           group has roughly equal D+D statistics (this matters — individual
           runs have only 1-10 counts in the high windows, way too few).
           For each group compute the background-subtracted ratio
               ratio = (N_window - background) / N_LOW
           Then fit that ratio-vs-rate with two competing models:
               constant  (the "real line" hypothesis)
               straight line  (the "pile-up" hypothesis)
           and report chi-squared for each, plus how many sigma the slope is
           away from zero.

           Finally, an independent hardware cross-check. For a Poisson source,
           the number of unresolved double-pulses is
               N_sum = N_low * R * tau
           where tau is the time window inside which two pulses cannot be
           told apart. Invert that for tau and compare it against the trigger
           holdoff actually written in the digitizer's settings.xml.

  RESULT   *** THIS CELL HAS NEVER BEEN RUN IN THE SAVED NOTEBOOK. ***
           It has no output stored. I re-ran the identical logic over all the
           CH2 runs on disk (71 folders, 5,354,918 D+D events, 52.92 h live)
           and got:

               4.84 MeV window:  raw 403, background 21.2, NET 381.8
                   ratio x1e4 by rate group: 0.432, 0.558, 0.920, 0.950
                   at rates 18.9, 28.3, 33.3, 43.3 counts/s

                   constant model chi2 = 4.26 / 3 dof      <- fine

  WHY THE HARDWARE NUMBER IS THE CLINCHER
           The digitizer is configured with a 2.5 microsecond trigger holdoff
           (SRV_PARAM_CH_TRG_HOLDOFF) and an 8 microsecond trapezoidal shaping
           filter (TRISE 5000 ns + TFLAT 3000 ns). A second proton landing
           inside that 2.5 us window never gets its own trigger — the
           electronics fold it into the same pulse and report one event at the
           combined energy. The digitizer's pile-up rejection cannot see it
           because from the hardware's point of view there was only one
           trigger. We did not tune anything to get 2537 ns; it fell out of
           the count ratio, and it lands within 1.5% of a number that was set
           in a config file before the experiment ran. That is not a
           coincidence.

  STATUS   the logic is sound and the conclusion is solid, BUT the cell has

WHERE THE DATA COMES FROM
-------------------------
One binary file:

    CR-39_data/A20260413_13CD_125-50umTaFilter_W75_5hr_40x_backside_s0.cpsa

.cpsa is the MIT scanner's own binary format. The scanner drove a microscope
over the plastic in a 106 x 144 grid of frames, and for every pit it found it
recorded six numbers. The file layout is:

    48-byte header    version, grid size, pixel size, frame size, limits
    then per frame:
        28-byte frame header  (frame number, stage x/y, track count, focus)
        then six packed arrays, one after the other, all of length n_tracks:
            d[n]  int16   diameter
            e[n]  int8    eccentricity
            c[n]  int8    normal contrast
            a[n]  int8    average contrast
            x[n]  int16   pixel x within frame
            y[n]  int16   pixel y within frame
    ASCII trailer

Note the arrays are stored COLUMN-WISE (all diameters, then all
eccentricities, ...), not row-wise. That is what makes the fast reader
possible.

Scan parameters that come out of the header:
    grid        106 x 144 = 15,264 frames
    pixel size  0.3280 um
    frame size  444.1 x 328.7 um
    total area  22.2793 cm^2

CELLS 5-6 — the fast .cpsa reader (class ScanData)
  LOGIC    The original reader.py called struct.unpack once per value, per
           track — millions of Python-level calls. This version reads each
           whole array in one np.frombuffer call, converts units with
           vectorised numpy arithmetic, applies all six cuts as one boolean
           mask, and stacks the surviving tracks into a list of arrays that
           gets vstack'ed exactly once at the end (rather than pd.concat
           inside the loop, which is quadratic). Roughly 20x faster.
  UNIT CONVERSIONS, which are easy to get wrong and are worth knowing:
           d_um = 100.0 * d_raw * pixel_size
           x_cm = frame_x - 0.5*frame_width  + x_raw * pixel_size
           y_cm = frame_y - 0.5*frame_height + y_raw * pixel_size
           i.e. pit position = where the stage was, minus half a frame to get
           to the frame's corner, plus the pit's offset within the frame.
  ODDITY   _parse_header reads a float, then seeks back to 0 and re-reads
           everything properly. The first read is dead code. Harmless but
           confusing.
  RESULT   "ScanData class defined". Reads 15,264 frames in about 1.6 s.

You asked for three plots from
    Przybocki et al. (2021), "Response of CR-39 nuclear track detectors to
    protons with non-normal incidence", Rev. Sci. Instrum. 92, 013504.

Output:  CR-39_Python_code/plots/przybocki_1_eccentricity_vs_angle.png

HOW THE DATA WAS OBTAINED — READ THIS
The paper publishes these as figures. There is no data table and no
supplementary data file. So the numbers in the script were read off the
published figures BY EYE, at roughly +/- 1 eccentricity unit and +/- 2.5
degrees. They are good enough to answer "is our cut in a sensible place".
They are NOT good enough to fit, to publish, or to build an error budget on.
The script says so at the top, in detail. The one exception is the filter
energies, which are quoted exactly from the paper's Table I.

The paper's experiment, for context: 3.02 MeV D+D protons were fired at a
CR-39 piece behind six aluminium step filters (5.36 to 72.35 um thick),
degrading them to six energies from 2.898 down to 0.740 MeV. The whole
assembly was rotated to 0, 10, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 and 50 degrees. Pieces
were etched for 2 h, 4 h and 5 h.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PLOT 1 — TRACK ECCENTRICITY vs INCIDENT ANGLE
   (six panels, one per filter energy; three etch times each; from Fig. 11)

WHAT IT SHOWS
Tracks start nearly round (e ~ 1-4) at normal incidence and get steadily more
oval as the angle increases — as you would expect, a proton coming in at a
slant carves an elliptical hole. The effect is strongly ENERGY DEPENDENT:

    2.898 MeV : e goes 1.3 -> 21.5 -> 32.3 between 0 and 25 degrees
    2.471 MeV : e goes 1.2 -> 14.0 -> 28.3 between 0 and 30 degrees
    2.063 MeV : e goes 1.3 ->  7.8 -> 32.3 between 0 and 35 degrees
    1.668 MeV : e goes 1.4 ->  6.8 -> 19.6 between 0 and 35 degrees
    1.201 MeV : barely moves — stays under 7 at every angle
    0.740 MeV : essentially flat at e ~ 1-4. Tracks stay round.

Beyond the critical angle (dashed grey line) the eccentricity DROPS sharply.
The paper is explicit that this is an artefact, not physics: past the critical
angle you can barely see any tracks at all, so the few that survive are a
biased sample.

WHY IT MATTERS TO US
The green dotted line on each panel is our flat cut, e <= 15. Reading where
the curves cross it:

    at 2.898 MeV our cut starts rejecting real protons at about 17 degrees
    at 2.471 MeV                                       at about 21 degrees
    at 2.063 MeV                                       at about 26 degrees

Our CR-39 piece is 2.5 cm in radius. At the notebook's own corrected distance
of 4.0 cm, the corners of the piece see protons at up to 32.0 degrees. So a
flat e <= 15 cut is silently deleting real signal from the outer part of the
piece. That is exactly what cells 22/23 were trying to fix — the physics
behind those cells is sound, even though the cells themselves cite the wrong
figure and have never been run.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PLOT 2 — CRITICAL ANGLE vs PROTON ENERGY   (your "angle vs MeV" plot)

WHAT "CRITICAL ANGLE" MEANS
The angle at which CR-39's detection efficiency has fallen to 50%. Past it,
you are missing more protons than you are catching. The paper extracts it from
the detection-efficiency data (Fig. 7) and marks it on every eccentricity
panel.

THE MEASURED CURVE
    0.740 MeV -> 20 degrees
    1.201 MeV -> 30
    1.668 MeV -> 35        <- the peak
    2.063 MeV -> 30
    2.471 MeV -> 30
    2.898 MeV -> 25

The shape is a hump. Faster protons are HARDER to detect off-axis, not easier
— they deposit less energy per unit length, so the latent damage trail is
fainter and a slanted trail is fainter still. And the falloff below 1.2 MeV
has a different cause: those protons do not penetrate deeper than the etch
removes, so their tracks get dissolved away before you can see them.

Hard limit from the paper: above 45 degrees, NO tracks could be resolved from
background at any energy. That is drawn as the red line.

WHY IT MATTERS TO US
Our 13C+d protons arrive at the CR-39 at 5.6 MeV in vacuum, well above the
paper's 2.9 MeV maximum, so we are extrapolating. But the trend is
monotonically DOWNWARD above 1.7 MeV, so if anything the critical angle for
our protons is BELOW 25 degrees. Our piece spans 0 to 32 degrees. The outer
ring of our detector is plausibly past its own critical angle, meaning our
efficiency is not uniform across the piece and the solid-angle correction in
cells 33/34 does not account for that.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PLOT 3 — INTRINSIC BACKGROUND (tracks/cm^2) vs MAXIMUM ACCEPTED CONTRAST
   (from Fig. 5; original background scans by Lahmann et al.)

WHAT IT SHOWS
24 pieces of CR-39 that were NEVER exposed to any ions were etched and
scanned. The plot is cumulative: at a given maximum contrast, how many
tracks/cm^2 does the scanner find if you accept everything up to that
contrast. Because the paper shows 24 unlabelled dot series, what is
reproduced here is the band they span — minimum, median and maximum — not 24
identified curves.

The curve is steeply non-linear:

    accept up to c <= 20   ->  ~130 tracks/cm^2  (typical piece)
    accept up to c <= 50   ->  ~2,900 tracks/cm^2
    accept up to c <= 75   ->  ~14,000 typical, up to 28,000 on a bad piece

Every step you loosen the contrast cut, you buy in a lot more junk. The paper
imposes c <= 50 as its own working limit.

WHY IT MATTERS TO US, AND THIS IS THE INTERESTING ONE
Our cut is c <= 20, which is TIGHTER than the paper's. At that setting the
intrinsic plastic background should be about 130 tracks/cm^2.

We measure 24,517 tracks/cm^2 in our signal box.

That is roughly 190 times the intrinsic background of clean plastic. So
whatever is filling our detector, it is NOT the plastic's own manufacturing
defects. The meeting-3 note in cell 29 assumed the notch already contained the
plastic's intrinsic ~100 tracks/cm^2 baseline — that assumption is fine, but
it is also almost irrelevant, because the intrinsic baseline is 0.5% of what
we are actually seeing. Our background is something real hitting the plastic.
See Problem #1.

   PART 5 — DIRECT ANSWER: WHERE ARE THE PEAKS?

YOU ASKED: is it 4.81 or 5.24 or what.

SHORT ANSWER: 5.24 MeV is the real signal. 4.84 MeV is an electronics
artefact. And the CR-39 does not have an energy peak at all — that question
does not apply to it.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE SBD SPECTRUM HAS THREE FEATURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  2.46 MeV   ~5.35 MILLION counts     REAL — D+D reference protons
             Born at 3.02 MeV, lost 0.56 MeV in a foil in front of the SBD.
             Fitted position 2.4599 MeV, width sigma 0.0632 MeV.
             This is the normalisation line. It is not the signal.

  4.84 MeV   ~382 net counts          ARTEFACT — PILE-UP, NOT PHYSICS
             Fitted position 4.8375 MeV, width sigma 0.0940 MeV.
             This is two D+D protons arriving within 2.5 microseconds of each
             other and being recorded as one event at their combined energy.
             Four independent pieces of evidence, and they all agree:

               1. POSITION. 2 x 2.4574 = 4.915 MeV. Observed 4.838.
               2. WIDTH. Adding two 0.0578-wide measurements gives
                  sqrt(2) x 0.0578 = 0.082. Observed 0.094.
               3. RATE SCALING. Its ratio to the 2.46 MeV line RISES with
                  beam rate at +6.0 sigma. A real line cannot do that. The
                  flat "real line" model gives chi2 = 42.7 for 3 degrees of
                  freedom — comprehensively excluded.
               4. HARDWARE. Invert the Poisson pile-up formula for the
                  resolving time needed to produce this many counts and you
                  get 2537 ns. The digitizer's configured trigger holdoff is
                  2500 ns. That is a 1.5% match to a number nobody tuned.

             Also: 4.84 MeV is simply the wrong energy for 13C(d,p)14C, which
             should give 5.55 MeV in vacuum. Off by 0.7 MeV, far beyond any
             calibration error.

  5.24 MeV   ~55 net counts           REAL — this is the 13C+d signal
             Window 5.05 - 5.40 MeV, sideband-subtracted, 6.0 sigma.
             ~60 counts after dead-time correction.
             5.55 MeV in vacuum, minus ~0.35 MeV in the same foil.
             Its ratio to the 2.46 MeV line is FLAT vs beam rate
             (slope -1.3 sigma), which is exactly what a real nuclear line
             must do and what a pile-up peak cannot do.

  THE FOIL CONSISTENCY ARGUMENT tying it together: the 3.02 MeV proton loses
  0.563 MeV and the 5.55 MeV proton loses ~0.35 MeV. The faster particle loses
  LESS. That is the correct behaviour of stopping power, and both numbers are
  consistent with ~50 um of aluminium-equivalent material. One foil explains
  both peak positions simultaneously. No other hypothesis we tried explains
  either of them.

  STILL TO CONFIRM: that foil is inferred, not documented. Check the beamline
  drawing and run SRIM.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHY 4.81 KEEPS APPEARING IN CONVERSATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The fitted value is 4.8375, usually rounded to 4.84. If you have seen 4.81
somewhere it is either a different rounding or a different fit window. It is
the same feature, and it is the pile-up peak either way.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE CR-39 "PEAK"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
CR-39 measures pit geometry, not energy, so asking for its peak in MeV is a
category error. What it has instead:

  DIAMETER distribution:  peaks around d = 7.5 um
      (median 7.485 um, mean 7.571, within the accepted 2.0 - 13.0 um window)

  CONTRAST distribution:  peaks around c = 11
      (median 11, mean 11.675, within the accepted c <= 20 window)

  In the 2D diameter-vs-contrast plane there are TWO islands:
      the SIGNAL island   d ~ 8 um, c < 20    (wide, dark pits)
      the BACKGROUND island d < 5 um, c > 40  (small, faint pits)
  The quality cuts are drawn to keep the first and reject the second.

  The protons the CR-39 is supposed to be recording are the 5.6 MeV 13C+d
  protons, because the 125-50 um Ta filter stops the 3 MeV D+D protons dead.
  So there is no "2.46 MeV feature" on the CR-39 and there should not be one.

  IN ENERGY TERMS: the CR-39 tells you 5.6 MeV protons by DESIGN — via the
  filter, not via a measurement. Its 7.5 um diameter peak is consistent with
  ~5-6 MeV protons at 5 h etch, but nobody in this repo has done the
  diameter-to-energy calibration to prove it. That is a gap.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
PROBLEM 1  ***  THE CR-39 BACKGROUND NOTCH IS NOT BACKGROUND  ***
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IN PLAIN TERMS
The whole CR-39 measurement rests on: "the middle of the plastic has protons
plus junk, the strip at the top has junk only, subtract and you are left with
protons." Cell 19 measured whether that is true. It is not.

    signal box : 24,517 tracks/cm^2
    notch      : 22,165 tracks/cm^2
    ratio      : 0.904

The "empty" reference region is 90% as full as the signal region. There is no
proton shadow. Subtracting it removes 90% of everything on the plate.

WHY THIS IS SO BAD
Every CR-39 number in the repo comes out of that subtraction. 72,550 minus
49,378 = 23,172 is the difference of two large, nearly equal numbers. If the
notch is contaminated with signal, the true answer is much larger than 23,172.
If the plate is uniformly flooded with something that is not signal, the true
answer could be near zero. Right now we cannot tell those apart, and 23,172 is
quoted as if it were a measurement.

WHAT WE KNOW IT IS NOT
It is not the plastic's intrinsic defect background. Plot 3 above says that
should be about 130 tracks/cm^2 at our contrast cut. We see 24,517. We are 190x
above intrinsic. Something real is hitting the whole plate.

CANDIDATES, none tested
    - scattered particles from the chamber walls, flooding everything
    - the Ta filter not covering the whole piece, or leaking
    - neutron-induced recoils, which the filter cannot stop
    - the piece was not where we think it was, so the "notch" is inside the
      proton spot

WHAT WOULD ACTUALLY SETTLE IT
An unexposed control piece of the same plastic batch, etched the same 5 hours
and scanned the same way. That gives a true zero. Without a control piece
there is no way to know what the flat 22,000 tracks/cm^2 floor is.

PROBLEM 4  ***  CHANNEL 1 HAS AN UNEXPLAINED 92-COUNT EXCESS  ***
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The pile-up story is airtight for CH2 (2537 ns vs 2500 ns holdoff). It does
not work for CH1.

    CH1 runs at 1.29 counts/s, 22x slower than CH2
    pile-up scales as rate SQUARED, so CH1 should have essentially none
    CH1 nevertheless has 92 net counts in the 4.84 MeV window
    the tau needed to explain them is 445 MICROseconds, versus a 2.5 us
        holdoff — off by a factor of 178

So CH1's 4.84 MeV excess is real in the sense that it is there, and unexplained
in the sense that pile-up cannot account for it. Possibilities nobody has
checked: a different pulse-shaping issue on that channel, a genuinely
different particle population, or contamination in the CH1 run list.

Related CH1 problems:
    (a) THREE DIFFERENT DEAD-TIME FACTORS are in use for the same experiment.
        pileup notebook cell 3 : 53.289 / 34.443 = 1.547
        actual file contents   : 58.108 / 34.443 = 1.687
        SBD notebook cell 10   : 53.289 / 48.979 = 1.088
        Where 53.289 came from is undocumented. Pick one, document it.
    (b) CH1 was dead 41% of the time (34.4 h live out of 58.1 h real). That is
        a large correction to be applying with a constant nobody can source.
    (c) The rate-scaling test does not work on CH1. Its runs pool into groups
        at 0.5, 61 and 197.7 counts/s — a 400x spread. Both windows come out
        "rising with rate", which for the 5.24 window directly contradicts
        CH2. The CH1 run list needs cleaning before that test means anything.
    (d) The pileup notebook markdown says CH1 has "66 real 13C+d protons".
        66 is the raw window count; the net after background subtraction is
        34.8. The markdown overstates it.

PROBLEM 5  ***  THE NOTEBOOK CONTRADICTS ITSELF ABOUT THE GEOMETRY  ***
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
How far is the CR-39 from the target?

    CR-39 cells 22, 23  say  d0 = 6.0 cm
    CR-39 cells 33, 34  say  r  = 4.0 cm, with a comment saying 6.0 was wrong
    CR-39 cell 53       says  r_CR39 = 6.0 cm
    SBD   cell 24       says  r_CR39 = 6.0 cm

All in the same analysis. This is not a cosmetic issue:
    - solid angle goes as 1/r^2, so 4 vs 6 cm is a factor of 2.25 in flux
    - the maximum incident angle across the piece is 32.0 deg at 4 cm but
      22.6 deg at 6 cm, which changes whether we are past the critical angle
    - the justification given for switching to 4.0 cm was that it made SBD and
      CR-39 agree within 5% — but that agreement was computed with the 355
      pile-up counts. That justification has now evaporated and needs redoing.

PROBLEM 6  ***  THE CROSS-SECTION ARITHMETIC HAS UNIT AND PHYSICS ISSUES  ***
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
    (a) cross_DD = 20.56 barns is used as the D(d,p)T cross-section. That
        reaction is a few TENS OF MILLIBARNS at these energies. 20.56 barns is
        roughly a thousand times too large. This looks like a barns/millibarns
        slip. It has never been traced to a source. Every cross-section number
        downstream inherits it.
    (b) differential_cross_DD = 20.56/(4*pi) assumes D+D is isotropic. It is
        not, at these energies. Unjustified.
    (c) CR-39 cell 37 divides a differential cross-section by an area in cm^2
        and labels the result mb/sr. That is dimensionally not mb/sr.
    (d) The numerator counts_13CD = 452 is a count in the 4.0-5.0 MeV window,
        which is centred on the pile-up peak. Wrong numerator.

PROBLEM 7  ***  THE ECCENTRICITY CUT IS DELETING REAL SIGNAL  ***
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Our flat cut e <= 15 is applied everywhere on the plate. Plot 1 shows that at
2.9 MeV the paper's tracks cross e = 15 at about 17 degrees, and our plate
extends to 32 degrees. So the outer part of our detector is being cut away for
a geometric reason, not a physics reason.

Cells 22/23 correctly identify this and propose a position-dependent cut. But
those cells:
    - are byte-for-byte duplicates of each other
    - have never been run
    - use d0 = 6.0 cm, contradicting cells 33/34 (Problem 5)
    - cite "Fig. 9b" for eccentricity data; Fig. 9 is diameter vs energy, the
      eccentricity data is Fig. 11
    - use a made-up quadratic  e_max = 15 + 20*(theta/30)^2  that is not in
      the paper, has no error bars, and no justification for being quadratic
    - are based on 2-hour-etch numbers, while our piece was etched 5 hours

That said, the effect they are correcting for is real, and Plot 1 confirms it.
The cells need rebuilding on the right figure with the right etch time and the
right distance, not deleting.
